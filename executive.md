# Executive Summary: AI-Generated Music in 2025

AI-generated music has moved from experiment to everyday reality. Major platforms now see meaningful volumes of synthetic tracks, creators are adopting AI tools at pace, and regulators are drafting rules for training, disclosure, and deepfakes. At the same time, measurement is fragmented: there is no single source of truth for how much AI music is on Spotify, YouTube, or TikTok, and most platform insights arrive through press statements and policy notes rather than open datasets. This summary distills what’s happening on platforms, what data exists for analysis, and the legal issues leaders should know—so teams can make informed product, licensing, and risk decisions.

## Platform signals and adoption

Public signals suggest high upload volumes with uneven listening impact. Deezer reported that roughly 28% of daily track deliveries are fully AI-generated—around 30,000 tracks per day—yet only about half a percent of total streams are AI-generated, and a large share of detected plays on synthetic uploads appear fraudulent. Deezer tags AI-generated content and excludes it from recommendations and editorial playlists. Spotify has cracked down on “spammy” uploads and artificial streaming—most notably in takedowns involving Boomy—although it does not expose per-track AI labels via an official dataset. YouTube requires creators to disclose realistic synthetic content, is expanding AI-content labels, and is moving toward provenance metadata, but these disclosure signals are not yet a comprehensive public dataset. TikTok’s impact reports highlight strong roles in discovery and “Add to Music App” saves, without breaking out AI-generated shares. Survey data from European collecting societies indicates creator-side adoption is real: in 2024, roughly a third of surveyed members used AI, rising above half among younger creators.

The practical takeaway is that uploads are surging while listening share remains modest and heavily curated away in some ecosystems. Platforms are actively policing manipulation and are building transparency obligations. For executives, expect growing volumes of AI submissions, limited recommendation exposure on mainstream platforms, and more explicit labeling or provenance controls over time.

## Data you can use today

Although platforms have not opened comprehensive AI-content datasets, several research corpora offer useful lenses on generation and usage. SONICS (Synthetic Or Not) provides end-to-end detection data for roughly ninety-seven thousand songs, including around forty-nine thousand synthetic tracks from Suno and Udio. It includes metadata such as generation source, genre and mood tags, lyrics features, and split information, with “real” songs referenced via YouTube IDs. AIMS Prompts (from KTH) captures how users interact with text-conditioned generation on Suno and Udio—over one hundred thousand songs between May and October 2024—with prompts, tags, and lyric practices, shared via URL lists and code rather than raw audio. SunoCaps pairs AI-generated tracks with emotion and prompt-alignment annotations, enabling evaluations of how well outputs reflect conditioning text.

Support datasets also help structure analysis even when they are not AI-specific. MusicCaps offers human-written captions that are often used to condition or evaluate generation, and large public Spotify track datasets provide audio features and popularity metrics that can be joined for trend analysis. In practice, combining SONICS and AIMS Prompts lets teams study growth and metadata distributions, prompt practices, and time-series adoption patterns, while selective API queries to YouTube and Spotify can augment signals where permitted by terms and law. The constraint to keep in mind is lawful access: data enrichment should respect platform terms, opt-outs, and anti-circumvention rules.

## Legal landscape and why it matters

The core legal issues span authorship, training data, output risks, and transparency. In the United States, copyright protection requires human authorship. Purely AI-generated works are generally not protectable; human contributions can be, if they are sufficiently creative and properly disclosed in registration. The question of whether training on copyrighted music is fair use remains unsettled for audio models. Earlier digitization cases recognized transformative indexing, while recent decisions narrowed the transformative analysis and emphasize market substitution. Labels have sued AI-music platforms over alleged unauthorized training and scraping, and industry movement suggests a trajectory toward licensing—with the EU already operating under text-and-data mining exceptions that include a rightsholder opt-out and the Japan regime allowing broader TDM for non-enjoyment purposes. The EU AI Act adds transparency obligations for general-purpose and generative models, including summaries of copyrighted training data and deepfake labeling.

Output risks are concrete and immediate. Direct copying or sampling can infringe sound recordings and compositions, and “substantial similarity” claims can arise when synthetic outputs reproduce protectable musical expression. “Style-of” generation is not protected as a category, but outputs that emulate distinctive and protectable elements can still trigger claims. Voice cloning engages rights of publicity and personality; US case law recognizes unauthorized commercial voice imitation as actionable, and new state laws, such as Tennessee’s ELVIS Act, explicitly protect voice against AI impersonation, with labor agreements requiring consent and compensation for digital replicas. Platform liability questions under the DMCA include the applicability of safe harbors to generative systems and anti-circumvention risks when scraping or stream-ripping bypasses access controls.

Collective management and royalties are adapting. US PROs accept registrations of partially AI-generated works where human authorship is substantial, while fully AI-generated works are not eligible. Neighbouring rights frameworks in the EU and UK may attach producer rights to sound recordings even without human performers, raising operational questions about who is remunerated and how collecting societies will treat AI-only phonograms. Consumer protection and transparency obligations are rising, with labeling and provenance systems becoming more common.

## Practical implications for teams

Executives should treat training data and provenance as governance priorities. Prefer licensed catalogs or opt-out-respecting sources, avoid scraping behind paywalls or technical access controls, and document dataset lineage, lawful access, and model improvements. For outputs, implement filters and fingerprinting to reduce memorization and detect regurgitation, maintain audit trails of prompts and edits, and prohibit unauthorized voice impersonation in user terms, with rapid takedown and staydown pathways for exact matches where feasible. In EU contexts, be ready to label synthetic audio and disclose AI usage; in US contexts, document human contributions for registrations and PRO submissions. Build consent workflows for voices, and align contracts to cover training, model improvement, and synthetic uses explicitly.

On measurement, set pragmatic KPIs even without perfect data: track submission volumes and acceptance rates for AI-generated content, label coverage and provenance adoption, takedown velocity and repeat infringer management, and the proportion of plays and revenue attributable to AI-assisted works. Use research datasets to benchmark detection and alignment, and consider research partnerships with platforms for aggregated statistics.

## Where this is heading

Over the next two years, expect more licensing deals between AI platforms and rightsholders, wider adoption of disclosure and provenance standards, and clearer—but stricter—training documentation requirements in the EU. Upload volumes will continue to rise, but mainstream recommendations may remain cautious, with synthetic catalogs living in parallel ecosystems or curated niches. Voice rights are likely to harden, driving consent-based models for cloning and synthetic performances. The executive challenge is to capture the creative and product upside—personalization, rapid iteration, new formats—while instituting disciplined data governance, lawful acquisition, and audience trust through transparent labeling and provenance.

This summary is informational and not legal advice. Laws and platform policies change quickly; teams should consult counsel, monitor active litigation and regulatory guidance, and update governance as obligations evolve.
